1. Radiographic Patterns: Bacterial vs. Viral
The primary distinction on imaging lies in how the inflammation manifests—whether it fills the alveolar spaces or thickens the interstitial tissue.

Bacterial Pneumonia (Alveolar Process): Typically presents with consolidation. This is an alveolar-filling process where inflammatory exudate replaces air, increasing lung density and obscuring the margins of adjacent airways and vessels.

Key Patterns: Lobar or segmental consolidation, the "air bronchogram" sign (air-filled bronchi remaining visible against dense, consolidated lung parenchyma), and the bulging fissure sign. 

Pathogen Links: Streptococcus pneumoniae and Klebsiella pneumoniae frequently display classic single-lobe consolidation. Staphylococcus aureus often presents with a patchy bronchopneumonia pattern.

Viral Pneumonia (Interstitial Process): Viruses tend to trigger an inflammatory reaction in the lung interstitium and alveolar walls rather than immediately filling the airspaces.

Key Patterns: Ground-glass opacities (GGO) and reticulonodular infiltrates. GGOs are hazy increases in lung density that do not obscure underlying bronchial walls or pulmonary vessels. 

Pathogen Links: COVID-19 typically presents with peripheral, subpleural, bilateral, and basal GGOs. Influenza often manifests with reticulonodular opacities.

Overlap Warning: In severe viral cases, GGOs can progress to extensive alveolar consolidation, making them radiographically indistinguishable from bacterial infections on an X-ray alone.

2. Correlation with Clinical Data and Microbiology
Because radiological patterns alone are rarely definitive, they must be fused with patient history and lab results to isolate the etiology.

Clinical Synergy: A dense lobar consolidation on an X-ray paired with acute onset, high fever, and purulent sputum strongly correlates with a typical bacterial etiology. Conversely, diffuse GGOs in a patient presenting with a gradual onset of dry cough, myalgias, and a normal white blood cell (WBC) count lean heavily toward a viral cause.

Microbiological Confirmation: Sputum and blood cultures remain the gold standard for diagnosing bacterial pneumonia, while RT-PCR is definitive for viral pneumonia. Notably, "atypical" bacteria (like Mycoplasma pneumoniae) often mimic viral imaging patterns (centrilobular nodules, peribronchovascular GGOs) and require targeted PCR or serology to accurately diagnose.

3. The Role of Biomarkers
Biomarkers provide quantifiable, objective data to differentiate etiologies, proving especially valuable when radiographic findings are equivocal or when attempting to identify a secondary bacterial infection superimposed on a viral pneumonia.

Procalcitonin (PCT): Highly specific to bacterial infections. PCT levels rise rapidly (within 6 hours) in response to bacterial endotoxins but remain low during viral illnesses. A cutoff of >0.25–0.5 ng/mL is generally indicative of a bacterial infection, while <0.1 ng/mL strongly suggests a viral etiology.

C-Reactive Protein (CRP): An acute-phase reactant that rises with systemic inflammation. While CRP is usually significantly higher in bacterial pneumonia (often >40–100 mg/L), it can also be elevated in severe viral infections, making it less specific than PCT.

Diagnostic Strategy: Combining these markers offers the best predictive power. Elevated CRP and PCT levels, alongside neutrophilia (high WBC count), have a high positive predictive value for bacterial infections. Rapidly declining PCT/CRP levels over 48 hours further correlate with a viral illness or successful source control.

Emerging Biomarkers: Newer proteins like Myxoma resistance protein 1 (MxA1) are currently being studied, as they rise significantly during viral infections rather than bacterial ones, offering a potential "viral-positive" blood marker.
